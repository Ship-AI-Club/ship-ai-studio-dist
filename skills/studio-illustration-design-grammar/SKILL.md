# Illustration Design Grammar

Use this skill to turn a project's brand and subject matter into a coherent family of original illustrations. It covers two related formats: **narrative posters** with dense detail and integrated lettering, and **character spots** with a simpler silhouette and selective detail. When the user asks to upgrade an existing illustration, preserve its brief and recognizable subject while increasing clarity, specificity, and finish.

## Reference and authorship

This grammar was distilled from visual observation of [Yonderism Studio's illustration portfolio](https://www.yonderism.com/#illustration) in October 2026. The observable traits include hand-drawn contours, flat color, expressive lettering, compact narrative scenes, decorative frames, and small discoveries within the image. The studio describes a process that starts with concept sketches and draws the typography by hand. These are **principles to adapt**, not assets or templates to copy.

The reference site reserves rights to its artwork and prohibits reproduction, modification, and use for AI training. Do not download, trace, reproduce, upload as an image-generation reference, or train on its artwork without permission. Create new characters, layouts, lettering, motifs, and color relationships from the user's project. If the user provides licensed references or their own artwork, follow their permissions and instructions.

### Observed visual DNA

Use this as an analysis of recurring choices, not a specification to duplicate:

| Trait | Rich posters | Simpler character spots |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Tall compositions, nested scenes, arches or decorative edges, and lively near-symmetry. | One expressive figure or small pair, a strong pose, and generous surrounding space. |
| Line | Dark, hand-drawn contours with fine interior description and repeated small marks. | The same drawn contour language, with far fewer interior marks. |
| Color | Bold flat fields mixing warm earthy tones with bright blue, green, pink, or violet; palettes change by piece. | A compact subset of colors with clear contrast between figure and ground. |
| Type | Custom display lettering forms part of the image; practical details occupy calmer panels. | Usually no major typography inside the spot; personality comes from gesture and props. |
| Story | A main event or scene surrounded by little characters, symbols, and place-specific clues. | A character's role or attitude communicated through expression, costume, and one or two props. |

The overall effect is playful and handmade, with the visual organization of a well-planned poster. Preserve that balance of exuberance and hierarchy when adapting the grammar.

## Route the request

1. **New system:** define a project-specific grammar, then make representative poster and character examples if the user requests artwork.
2. **New illustration:** use the project's existing grammar or establish a compact one, choose the appropriate format, and produce the requested deliverable.
3. **Fidelity upgrade:** inspect the supplied piece and its intended size; identify the weakest layers; revise the artifact itself if editable. A critique alone is insufficient when the user asks for an upgrade.

Use the tools and file formats appropriate to the deliverable. For code-native artwork, edit SVG, canvas, HTML/CSS, or other existing source directly. For a bitmap illustration or image edit, use the available image-generation or graphics workflow. Do not require a particular app or medium. If the user has not supplied a brand system, make a sensible starting system and state the assumptions briefly.

## Set the project fingerprint first

Extract these from the brief, existing brand, audience, and placement. Choose defaults only where the project is silent. Keep the decisions together as a short **illustration spec** so subsequent pieces stay related.

| Decision | Specify |
| --- | --- |
| Story world | The project's places, people, objects, rituals, and visual metaphors. |
| Mood | Two or three precise adjectives, such as curious, rugged, or celebratory. |
| Silhouette language | Dominant shapes, proportions, and degree of exaggeration. |
| Ink | Dark contour color, primary-to-secondary line ratio, and how imperfect the line may be. |
| Palette | Background, dominant, support, accent, and any reserved colors. |
| Detail vocabulary | Three to eight original motifs, textures, or props tied to the subject. |
| Lettering | Display-letter character and how it relates to the illustration. |
| Density | Where detail gathers and where the eye can rest. |
| Use cases | Poster, cover, editorial, social, web, merchandise, or small icon. |

**Suggested starting ranges, not claims about the reference artwork:** use one dark ink color, a dominant background, two or three supporting colors, and one accent. Make primary contours about twice as visually strong as internal details. Check the palette in grayscale and at intended display size; revise rather than forcing a numerical rule that fails the image.

Turn the fingerprint into usable constraints. For example, an independent bookshop might use ink blue, parchment, rust, and leaf green; stacks, bookmarks, moths, and reading lamps as its motif set; and tall, slightly uneven lettering. A marine research project would keep the drawing grammar but replace every one of those choices with its own palette, forms, and visual story.

## Shared grammar

Apply these principles to both formats. Let the project's fingerprint decide their exact expression.

- **A story before ornament.** Write a one-sentence scene or visual metaphor. Every major prop should support that idea, reveal context, or help orientation.
- **Readable first, discoverable second.** The silhouette, focal subject, and headline must work at thumbnail size. Small jokes, textures, and secondary narratives reward closer viewing.
- **Ink holds the family together.** Use a confident outer contour, quieter interior lines, and selective hatching or marks. Slight asymmetry and varied curves should feel intentional, not careless.
- **Color sits in clear shapes.** Prefer flat or nearly flat fills with limited texture. Separate adjacent forms by value, hue, ink, or negative space.
- **Type belongs to the composition.** For a poster, shape the main words to echo its scene and frame. Keep dates, locations, and other functional copy legible and proofread.
- **Detail has a hierarchy.** Concentrate high-frequency marks in one or two zones. Give the main subject clean edges and preserve open areas. Do not fill every gap.
- **The frame has a job.** Borders, panels, ribbons, corner ornaments, and repeated motifs should organize information or deepen the setting, not merely decorate.
- **Irregularity is controlled.** Repeat line behavior, shape logic, and color choices across pieces; vary exact placements and small marks so the work retains a drawn feel.

| Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Draw a project-specific prop that explains the scene. | Add random tiny objects solely to make the image look busy. |
| Give dense areas a contrasting quiet area. | Apply equal detail and line weight everywhere. |
| Build a new border from the project's own world. | Reuse a recognizable frame, character, or lettering arrangement from the reference. |
| Redraw display lettering to suit each composition. | Distort small functional text until it is hard to read. |

## Mode A: narrative poster

Use for events, covers, campaigns, and editorial hero art where viewers can spend time exploring.

### Composition

1. Start with three thumbnail layouts before detailing. Establish a primary title, central scene, secondary information zone, and an outer boundary or visual edge.
2. Give the image a clear reading path: **title → main scene → practical information → small discoveries**. The path may curve or spiral but should remain easy to follow.
3. Build depth through overlapping foreground props, middle-ground action, and a simpler background. Scale shifts can be playful if the visual hierarchy stays clear.
4. Let a frame, arch, ribbon, illustrated object, or landscape shape hold the composition. Use near symmetry where it strengthens a poster; break it in a few places for energy.
5. Reserve a quiet field around essential text. Dense linework behind small copy reduces fidelity even when the drawing itself is skilled.

### Detail pass

- Give the hero scene specific evidence of place or purpose: tools, plants, signage, clothing, terrain, weather, architecture, or artifacts drawn from the user's project.
- Repeat two or three motifs at different scales so the piece feels designed rather than randomly filled.
- Add secondary action only where it clarifies the story or creates a worthwhile discovery.
- Use hatching, stipple, small pattern, or print-like texture selectively to describe form and material. Keep the largest shapes clean.
- Compare the result at full size and at roughly one-quarter size. If the headline, subject, or event information disappears, simplify the competing marks.

## Mode B: character spot

Use for avatars, editorial spots, sticker-like art, icons, supporting web visuals, and small social placements.

### Composition

1. Build one unmistakable silhouette with a distinct pose or gesture. The character should be recognizable before facial features or texture are added.
2. Give it one or two meaningful props that carry the project story. A tiny environment cue or ground shadow may help; a full poster background usually will not.
3. Exaggerate proportions consistently: decide what is large, small, soft, angular, or expressive. Keep that anatomy logic across a set of characters.
4. Use a quieter palette and more open space than in poster mode. A contrasting shape or simple field can make the character pop without a border.

### Detail pass

- Put fine detail at the face, hands, key prop, or another identity-bearing feature. Keep broad body areas calm.
- Add costume, material, or texture marks that explain the character, not generic scratches.
- Test on its actual background and at its smallest intended size. Remove interior lines that merge into noise.
- For a series, vary poses and props while preserving contour behavior, proportions, eye treatment, palette logic, and texture density.

## Fidelity upgrade loop

Do not equate fidelity with more marks. Improve the weakest layer first and stop when additional detail reduces comprehension.

| Layer | Upgrade question | Effective change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concept | Is the scene specific to this project? | Replace a generic symbol with a relevant action, object, or setting. |
| Silhouette | Is the subject clear at a glance? | Adjust pose, scale, overlap, and negative space. |
| Structure | Do parts feel intentionally constructed? | Correct perspective, anatomy logic, joints, and object connections. |
| Story | Does each secondary element add meaning? | Add a few project-specific props or micro-scenes. |
| Line | Is there a visible contour hierarchy? | Strengthen the outside edge; quiet interior marks. |
| Color | Do values separate the important forms? | Rebalance the dominant field and accent placement. |
| Texture | Does texture describe material or mood? | Add targeted pattern; remove indiscriminate noise. |
| Type | Does lettering feel integrated and read correctly? | Refine display shapes and proofread functional text. |
| Finish | Does it hold up in the intended medium? | Fix tangencies, cramped text, stray gaps, clipping, and export quality. |

Make a first pass for concept and hierarchy, a second for construction and storytelling, and a final pass for line, color, texture, typography, and production. Reassess at each stage rather than adding all possible detail.

## Quality gates

Before delivery, inspect the actual artifact at thumbnail, intended, and close-view sizes. For a series, compare pieces side by side.

- **Three-second read:** Can someone identify the subject, mood, and primary message quickly?
- **Project specificity:** Could this piece be mistaken for an unrelated brand? If so, strengthen the subject-specific details.
- **Hierarchy:** Are the title or character and the practical information clearly ordered?
- **Controlled density:** Do the busiest areas have nearby visual rest?
- **Line consistency:** Do primary and secondary marks obey the same logic throughout?
- **Accessibility:** Is essential text legible and does the main subject separate from its background?
- **Originality:** Are characters, motifs, lettering, and composition newly developed for the project?
- **Production:** Are dimensions, crop, bleed or safe area, transparency, and file format appropriate to the placement?

Revise any failed gate that matters to the user's use case. Report remaining limitations plainly.

## Deliverable behavior

When making a **style guide**, provide the project fingerprint, both format rules, a small palette with roles, line and texture rules, motif vocabulary, typography guidance, and a few clear do/don't examples. When making **artwork**, deliver the requested files and a short explanation of the decisions that tie them to the system. When **upgrading artwork**, identify the most important before/after changes and verify the actual result visually. Do not substitute a prompt or prose description for an artifact the user asked you to create.

If the request is open-ended, make a credible first version rather than waiting for every brand choice. Ask only for information that would materially change the result and cannot be inferred from the project.
